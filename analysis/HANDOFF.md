# Why a bare `Name:` works

Generated by `analysis/handoff.py`.

**5,169** bare-handoff turns followed by a generation, **834** distinct after removing duplicated stories. Uptake = the generation does not switch to a different speaker tag in its first 80 characters.

## Established names against first appearances

| name | handoffs | voice held | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| already used earlier in the session | 547 | **90.7%** | 87.9–92.8% |
| first appearance in the session | 287 | **85.0%** | 80.4–88.7% |
| all | 834 | **88.7%** | 86.4–90.7% |

Gap: **+5.7 points** in favour of established names (two-proportion z = 2.46, p = 0.014).

**Both things are true, and the larger one is the convention.** A name the model has never seen still holds **85.0%** of the time. There is no referent for it anywhere in the session — the model cannot be tracking a cast member it has not met — so that share is `Name:` working as a formatting template.

On top of that, establishment adds a real but small **+5.7 points** (p = 0.014). So the model does attend to who is already in the room; it just is not where most of the effect comes from.

This makes the 89.7% headline in `CUES.md` a weaker result about partnership than it first looked. It was offered as the cleanest evidence that the model was tracking the scene. Most of it is the strength of a formatting convention, and the scene-tracking component is a few points on top.

## Does more prior context help?

If the model is tracking a cast, more session text before the handoff should mean better uptake. Bucketed by how much text preceded it.

| prior session text | handoffs | voice held | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 0–2,000 chars | 27 | **85.2%** | 67.5–94.1% |
| 2,000–8,000 chars | 42 | **81.0%** | 66.7–90.0% |
| 8,000–25,000 chars | 85 | **85.9%** | 76.9–91.7% |
