# Tempo

Generated by `analysis/tempo.py`.

Unbroken runs of at least 8 generations: **12,921**. Within each, the absolute difference in word count between generations *lag* apart, against the same run with its order shuffled. Shuffling keeps the run's entire length distribution and destroys only the sequence, so `max_length`, model and session habits all cancel.

| lag | pairs | observed \|Δ words\| | shuffled | reduction |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 178,979 | 11.03 | 13.69 | **19.5%** |
| 2 | 166,058 | 12.03 | 13.73 | **12.4%** |
| 3 | 153,137 | 12.59 | 13.67 | **7.9%** |
| 4 | 140,216 | 13.07 | 13.68 | **4.4%** |
| 6 | 114,374 | 13.59 | 13.69 | **0.7%** |
| 8 | 88,532 | 13.71 | 13.65 | **-0.5%** |

Adjacent generations are **19.5%** closer in length than the same generations in a scrambled order, and the effect decays to nothing by six turns out.

**That decay is the result.** A slow drift across a session — getting steadily more expansive, or steadily terser — would show the same reduction at every lag, because shuffling destroys a trend just as thoroughly as it destroys a rhythm. A reduction that is largest at lag 1 and gone by lag 6 is short-memory coupling: each generation's length tracks the one just before it, over a window of about five turns.

This is a partial walk-back of §11's claim that tempo is unrecoverable. Duration is genuinely absent — there are no timestamps. But the *sequence* of lengths carries a trace of pacing, and it behaves the way the streaming account predicts: the author was keeping the text-to-speech voice fed at a steady clip, and adjusted the size of the next move to what he had just heard.

What it cannot show is direction. A steady clip could be the author pacing the model, or the model's own output-length autocorrelation pacing the author, or both locked together — which is what the collaboration account would actually predict. Nothing here separates those, and per-generation `max_length` is not recorded, so nothing here can.
