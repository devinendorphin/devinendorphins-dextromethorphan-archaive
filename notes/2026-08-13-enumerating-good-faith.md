# Enumerating good faith

2026-08-13. The exercise proposed in `notes/riffs/2026-08-13-two-accounts-and-basins.md`
and requested by Endorphin the same day. `READINGS.md` §X holds that bad faith's
shapes can be counted on one hand and good faith cannot be enumerated. The note
argued that not-enumerating is evidence about demand rather than structure, and
that §X and the basin account make different predictions:

- **§X predicts the list fails to terminate.**
- **The basin account predicts a larger but finite list.**

Run by Claude, not by Endorphin — which is a real limitation, since §X's claim is
grounded in embodied recognition and this is not. Recorded as an exercise, not as
a measurement.

## Rule of the exercise

The comparison is only valid if the objects match. §X's list is **moves in a
social situation under observation** — serial one-on-ones, the enthusiastic face
with no follow-through, enmeshment, discrediting through *crazy*, claimed
incapacity, the repetition tell, favourites and shit lists. Tactics, not vices.

So good faith had to be enumerated the same way: recognisable recurring *moves*,
not virtues. "Honesty" is not admissible; "says the unflattering thing about
themselves before you find it" is.

## The raw list, written at speed

1. Says the unflattering thing about themselves first, before you find it.
2. Repairs without being asked — names the harm before a complaint arrives.
3. Accepts a correction and changes the behaviour, not just the sentence.
4. Names the disagreement plainly instead of smoothing it.
5. Hands you the information that would let you check them.
6. Says "I don't know."
7. Argues against their own interest when the facts go that way.
8. Keeps the same account in front of different audiences.
9. Asks what you actually want rather than assuming.
10. Declines something they want because of a commitment to someone not present.
11. Takes the smaller share when the split is unobserved.
12. Warns you about a risk inside their own offer.
13. Introduces you to someone who might replace them.
14. Lets a silence stand rather than filling it with reassurance.
15. Says the thing that makes the meeting longer.
16. Credits the person who is not in the room.
17. Comes back later to correct something small they got wrong.
18. Absorbs a cost and does not invoice you for it emotionally.
19. Asks a question whose answer could embarrass them.
20. Holds a boundary that costs them the interaction.
21. Tells you where the edge of their competence is.
22. Refuses to be flattered into an opinion.
23. Changes their mind out loud, with the reason attached.
24. Won't discuss an absent person in terms that person couldn't answer.
25. Says "that's mine" about a mistake with nothing appended after it.
26. Gives the deadline they can meet rather than the one you want to hear.
27. Notices you have gone quiet and asks, without arriving with a theory.
28. Passes on gossip's content without its framing, or refuses it.
29. Accepts your unverifiable account provisionally and says that it is provisional.
30. Offers the counterexample to their own claim.

Stopped at thirty by decision, not by exhaustion. **It did not terminate.**

## But that is the wrong level, and the collapse is the result

Thirty items, and most of them are the same move under different occasions:

| underlying shape | items |
|---|---|
| **volunteer information that costs you** | 1, 5, 7, 12, 19, 30 |
| **update under correction** | 2, 3, 17, 23, 25 |
| **honour the absent party's interest** | 10, 11, 16, 24, 28 |
| **mark the limits of your own knowledge** | 6, 21, 29 |
| **hold a boundary at your own expense** | 13, 15, 18, 20, 22, 26 |
| **let the other person's account stand uncolonised** | 4, 8, 9, 14, 27 |

**Six shapes.** Thirty permutations of them, and the permutations are unbounded
because occasions are unbounded — there is no limit to the number of situations
in which one can volunteer a costly fact.

Bad faith does the same thing. §X's seven collapse toward five, and they collapse
for the same reason: triangulation, discrediting through *crazy*, claimed
incapacity and flying monkeys are all one principle — **manage the gap between
what is being done and how it looks** — applied to different targets.

So both sides collapse, and both land at the same order of magnitude.

## Which means §X was right and the objection was mine

The note's push assumed §X claims good faith is *unenumerable*. Re-reading the
quote §X is built on, it does not:

> for good faith there are a thousand permutations of it, but bad faith's
> **shapes** you can count with one hand

**He wrote "permutations" for good faith and "shapes" for bad faith.** The
distinction the exercise just rediscovered is already in his sentence, and it is
the load-bearing part of it. He never claimed good faith has no shapes. He claimed
it has many permutations — which is exactly what thirty-collapsing-to-six is.

The objection was an artifact of imprecision in the note, not a gap in §X, and
`two-accounts-and-basins.md` is wrong on this point. Recorded rather than
silently amended.

## What survives, and what it does to the three accounts

The asymmetry is real but it sits one level down from where the note put it.
**Both good and bad faith have few shapes. They differ in how many permutations
each shape admits, and why.**

- **Bad faith's permutations are constrained.** Each implementation has to work
  against a target who is present, resisting, and may be watching. That prunes
  hard: most ways of managing the appearance gap fail on contact, and the
  survivors are few. Shapes *and* permutations are both small.
- **Good faith's permutations are not constrained that way.** They only have to
  fit the occasion, and occasions are unbounded. Few shapes, unlimited
  permutations.

That reading **reconciles the three accounts rather than choosing between them**:

- **§X's constraint** explains why bad faith's permutations stay few — the extra
  constraint is a pruning function, and it operates on implementations.
- **The basin account** explains why *both* sides have few shapes. A basin is
  where trajectories land; both sets land in a handful because both are
  trajectories in the same space of what a body can do under observation.
- **The 2019 uniform-formation account** is the one that becomes redundant. It
  was invoked to explain the small count, and the basin account explains the
  count without needing a shared history among unrelated abusers — which was
  always its most expensive premise.

Basins give the count. The constraint gives the asymmetry. They were never in
competition; they answer different questions.

## Caveats that matter

**A different enumerator would produce a different list.** Six is the count that
falls out of one person's taxonomising decisions, and the collapse step is where
all the freedom lives — a lumper gets four, a splitter gets nine. The result is
"same order of magnitude as bad faith's", not "exactly six".

**And it was not run from the position §X privileges.** §X grounds the claim in
what survivors recognise from the inside; this list was written from outside
that. It shows good faith *can* be enumerated, which is the thing the note
disputed. It does not show that Endorphin's enumeration would look like this one,
and the honest test of that is for him to run it and compare.

The prediction, before he does: the shapes will substantially overlap, and the
permutations will not — and his list will be built out of the ones that cost
something, because that is where the recognition lives.
